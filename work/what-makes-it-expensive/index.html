What makes it feel expensive?

A five-round game: pick the product card that feels more expensive, then see how strong the evidence behind each design rule really is.

TYPE
Interactive experiment, live on the web
ROUNDS
Five pairs of one product card, one change each
DATA
None collected; picks stay in the visitor’s browser
MY ROLE
Research, writing, design and build
THE QUESTION

Do premium design rules actually hold up?

Every designer knows the rules for making something look expensive: no discounts, lots of space, elegant type, round prices, no currency sign. I wanted to know how much of that is evidence and how much is habit.

So I built a game. Each round shows the same made-up candle twice, changing one thing. You pick, then see the convention, the research behind it, and an honest rating of how strong that research is.

THE FIVE RULES

And how strong the evidence is.

  1. 01No visible discountFairly strong. Frequent price deals go with lower brand equity across brands (Yoo, Donthu & Lee, 2000), though it’s a link from a survey, not an experiment.
  2. 02Generous white spaceA learned code. White space means prestige because of its history in modernist design, and its meaning varies across cultures (Pracejus, Olsen & O’Guinn, 2006).
  3. 03Thin, spaced serif typeReal, but it depends. Typeface design shapes impressions along six dimensions (Henderson, Giese & Cote, 2004); there’s no single expensive font.
  4. 04Round pricesMixed. Round prices “feel right” for emotional buys in five studies (Wadhwa & Zhang, 2015), but a 2018 replication didn’t clearly reproduce it.
  5. 05No currency signMixed. Diners spent about 8% more without dollar signs in one restaurant study (Yang, Kimes & Sessarego, 2009); the peer-reviewed version found no significant effect.
THE FINDING

Five rules, one with fairly strong support.

5CONVENTIONS TESTED
1WITH FAIRLY STRONG EVIDENCE
2WITH MIXED EVIDENCE

“Looks expensive” is mostly a shared language: worth speaking fluently, and worth testing before you bet a launch on it.

HOW IT’S BUILT

Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, so it loads instantly. Each round changes exactly one thing on the card, the left and right order is shuffled, and every claim links to its source. Nothing is tracked.
